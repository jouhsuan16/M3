Reflections on Working Together

A fuller account of how we build consensus, work through disagreement, and continue moving together without forcing sameness.

How We Learned to Work Together

Over time, our way of working together has gradually evolved, particularly in terms of how and where we reach consensus.

At the beginning, we mainly relied on online text-based discussions to build a shared language and develop our manifesto. During this stage, we spent a significant amount of time understanding each other’s ideas and attempting to identify a common direction.

Later, we found that face-to-face communication was more efficient. It allowed us to understand each other more directly and in real time, which in turn supported more immediate thinking and discussion, helping us move toward consensus more quickly.

Throughout this process, we encountered a key challenge: how to balance different working styles and ways of thinking. At times, it was difficult to continue progressing without full agreement. At the same time, because we did not want to force consensus, the process could feel slow and uncertain.

To address these challenges, we gradually accepted that disagreement is not something that needs to be resolved, but rather an integral part of the process itself. Instead of attempting to unify all perspectives, we allowed different viewpoints to coexist.

We also began to support each member in developing their ideas through experimentation. This allowed individuals to construct and test their own lines of thought, making them more legible and open to discussion. Each member was given space to explore their own direction first, after which the different outcomes were brought together for collective discussion and synthesis.

Working Through Disagreement

In our collective, conflict is not treated as something to be avoided, but as a natural and necessary part of the working process. When disagreements arise, we do not immediately seek to determine who is right. Instead, we focus on ensuring that different perspectives are fully expressed. Conflict is not understood as opposition, but as the coexistence of multiple ways of thinking.

In terms of practice, we adopt several strategies to navigate disagreements. Firstly, we allow additional time for discussion rather than rushing toward immediate decisions. By delaying closure, we create space for different viewpoints to develop more fully. Secondly, each member is encouraged to develop their ideas independently through experimentation, rather than attempting to reach alignment at an early stage. This allows ideas to be tested and refined before comparison or synthesis. Thirdly, when decisions are eventually made, minority perspectives are not erased. Instead, they are documented and remain visible for future reference and discussion.

Through these approaches, we gradually came to understand conflict as a productive resource for thinking, rather than a factor that undermines collaboration or relationships. More importantly, we recognize that maintaining a healthy collaborative relationship does not require the absence of conflict, but rather the ability to continue working together through disagreement while still moving the project forward.

For example, in the early stage of the project, Vayne proposed certain game prompt ideas, while Vicky felt they were too direct and lacked subtlety. Vayne accepted this feedback, and the group collectively rethought how to make the prompts more indirect and engaging. In another instance, Vicky and Joy initially developed relatively complex game mechanics. Vayne raised concerns that such complexity might reduce accessibility for users interacting with a lightweight web-based experience. After discussion, Joy and Vicky simplified parts of the system. Although Vayne still felt further simplification was possible, the final outcome represented a balanced solution acceptable to all members.
